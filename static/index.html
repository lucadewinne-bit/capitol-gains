Investing, in plain English

Everything below is education, not advice. Hover or tap any underlined term anywhere in the app for a quick definition.

What is a stock?

A stock (or “share”) is a tiny slice of ownership in a company. If you own one share of Apple, you own a very small piece of Apple. Its price moves all day as people trade it — up when more people want to buy, down when more want to sell. You make money if you sell for more than you paid (or through dividends); you lose money if the price drops and you sell for less.

What is the S&P 500?

The S&P 500 is a list of ~500 of the biggest U.S. companies (Apple, Microsoft, etc.), combined into one number. When people say “the market was up today,” they usually mean this. You can’t buy the S&P 500 itself, but funds like SPY or VOO copy it almost exactly. Historically it has averaged roughly 7–10% a year over decades — including some brutal years of -20% or worse along the way. That average is the yardstick this app measures everything against.

What is an ETF / index fund?

An ETF is a basket of many stocks that trades like a single stock. Buying one share of XLV, for example, spreads your money across dozens of healthcare companies at once. That’s why the sector tags in this app list ETFs as research starting points: they let you act on a sector idea (“defense spending is rising”) without betting everything on one company being the winner.

Risk and return — the honest version

There is no such thing as “beats the market, but can’t lose money.” Higher expected return is payment for accepting a bigger chance of losses. A single stock can drop 50% and stay there for years — or go to zero. The S&P 500 spreads that risk across 500 companies, which is why it’s so hard to beat: most professional fund managers fail to beat it over 10+ years. Any strategy in this app should be judged against simply buying the index, which is exactly what the My Monthly Plan tab measures.

Diversification

Diversification means not putting all your money in one stock or one sector. A common beginner-friendly structure people use: most of the monthly money into a broad index fund, and only a slice (for example 10–20%) into individual ideas from research like this. That way one bad pick can’t sink you. How you split is your call — the point is that the split, not the picks, does most of the protecting.

Dollar-cost averaging (what your monthly plan does)

Investing the same amount every month, no matter what the market is doing, is called dollar-cost averaging. It automatically buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when they’re high, and it protects you from the classic mistake of investing a lump sum right before a crash. Your monthly plan is already this strategy — the only question is what each month’s money buys.

Why congressional trades are public (the STOCK Act)

Since 2012, the STOCK Act requires members of Congress to publicly disclose their stock trades within 45 days, because they often know about upcoming laws, contracts, and investigations before markets do. This app reads those official filings directly. Two honest caveats: the disclosure lag means you’re seeing trades up to 45 days late, and amounts are reported in ranges (like “$15,001–$50,000”), so totals here are estimates. Members also trade for boring reasons — retirement rebalancing, a spouse’s job — not only because they know something.

How to read this app

Signals ranks tickers by attention in the data and shows its math openly: up to 40 points for congressional buying, 30 for policy activity in the sector, 30 for price momentum vs the S&P 500. Congress Trades is the raw official data behind it. Legislation and News tell you why a sector is moving. My Monthly Plan is the scoreboard: it compares your actual choices against “what if I’d just bought the index” — if that comparison stays negative for a long time, the data is telling you something important.
